# Report structure

A results readout that survives forwarding. The reader is usually not the person who bought the
automations, so every claim has to stand up without the meeting that produced it.

## Section order

1. **Headline.** The delivered result and the period it was earned in, together. Two large
   figures at most: one outcome, one efficiency. Under each, one line on how it was counted.
2. **What it delivered.** A short strip of supporting counts, each labelled with its own window.
   One chart, built from the data rather than pasted as an image. Then the gaps that are still
   open, stated as opportunities rather than failures.
3. **Where the next gain sits.** One recommendation, the one actually discussed with the
   customer. Name the others in a single sentence. Include how it would be switched on and what
   would be measured, with no dates or durations promised.
4. **Evidence and comparison.** Where similar workspaces got to, if you have permission to
   reference them. Never include a reference from the report's own recipient.
5. **What the next measurement will show.** Metric names and definitions, no figures. This is
   what converts a one-off document into a standing measurement.
6. **Appendices.** Source screenshots or exports, captions free of numbers.

## Headline construction

Lead with the number the customer earned, not the number they could earn. A projection belongs
on page three, under a heading that says it is a projection.

Where a conservative floor exists at the source, it is a subline under the headline, never the
headline itself. Where no floor exists, omit it rather than deriving one.

Under each headline figure, one line answering "how do you know". `Counted from logged activity,
not projected` does more work than another adjective.

## Efficiency figures

Prefer a measured figure over a modelled one even when the modelled one is larger. A recovered
time figure derived from logged activity can be reconciled by the customer; one derived from
task volume multiplied by an assumed handling time cannot.

When projected savings are trivial, minutes per week rather than hours, do not build a headline
block from them and do not derive your own. Use a second outcome figure instead and carry the
efficiency story in prose.

## What not to include

- Pricing, credit consumption, margin, or commercial terms. Those follow through the account
  owner, and a results readout that mentions them becomes a negotiation document.
- Counts of recruiters, seats or users, unless a single authoritative source exists.
- Any "zero so far" statistic. If a capability has not been used, leave it out rather than
  printing a dash next to it.
- Testimonials or references from the recipient's own organisation.
- Third-party logos, brand assets, or partner marks you do not have written permission to
  redistribute. Build the document in the recipient's own branding with assets they supplied.

## Framing for a workspace that started recently

A short running period is an advantage, not a caveat. State it plainly in the headline and let
the reader do the annualisation themselves. A result earned in five months reads as momentum; the
same result spread across twelve reads as a plateau.

Where attribution coverage is partial, say the figure is a floor and that the true number is
higher. Understating in the customer's favour costs nothing and buys the credibility of
everything else in the document.
